Why Dubai Brands Are Sleeping on the Africa Opportunity

Purple & Co July 27, 2026 7 min read

Ask a Dubai founder where they'll expand next and you'll hear the same three answers: Saudi, then maybe Qatar, then "eventually" India. Africa rarely makes the list — which is strange, because for a huge number of these businesses, Africa isn't even "next." It's already downstairs.

Over 25,000 African-owned companies are registered with the Dubai Chamber of Commerce today, using the city as their global base. UAE-Africa bilateral trade hit roughly $107 billion in 2024, and UAE investment into African economies has topped $118 billion since 2020 — making the UAE the fourth-largest foreign investor on the continent, behind only China, the EU and the US. The corridor isn't emerging. It's built, funded, and sitting mostly unused by the marketing and digital teams who could be capitalising on it.

The gap isn't opportunity. It's who's building for it.

Government and infrastructure money moved first. DP World has spent the last several years expanding ports and logistics terminals across the continent, cutting shipping delays and connecting African exporters to global supply chains. The UAE has signed Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreements with Kenya, Mauritius and the Republic of Congo, stripping out tariffs and non-tariff barriers market by market. The financial and logistics rails are in place.

What hasn't followed at the same pace is the brand, e-commerce and digital layer that actually turns a trade corridor into revenue for a mid-sized business. A freight company can move your product to Lagos or Nairobi. Almost nothing about that supply chain tells you how to build a checkout flow, a payments stack, or a marketing funnel that a Kenyan or Nigerian customer will actually trust and use.

The infrastructure to reach Africa from Dubai already exists. The businesses that win won't be the ones who discover the corridor — they'll be the ones who build for the customer on the other end of it.

What "building for Africa" actually means

This is where most GCC brands get it wrong: they treat Africa expansion like a Saudi expansion — translate the site, adjust the currency, ship the same funnel. Africa's digital economy doesn't run on the same assumptions.

Mobile money isn't a payment option. It's the default.

Across much of sub-Saharan Africa, mobile money accounts already outnumber traditional bank accounts. A checkout page built around cards and Apple Pay — the standard GCC playbook — quietly excludes a huge share of the market before a single ad is clicked. An e-commerce build for Lagos or Nairobi has to treat mobile money as the primary rail, not a secondary option bolted on later.

The market is bigger and faster-moving than most pitch decks assume

Africa's digital commerce market, led by Egypt, Kenya, Morocco, Nigeria and South Africa, is projected to reach $72 billion in 2026. South Africa's e-commerce sector alone accelerated to a 38% annualised growth rate in 2025, driven by mobile shopping, faster delivery and trusted local payment options. This isn't a frontier market moving cautiously — it's compounding faster than most GCC verticals, with less competition for attention per dollar spent.

Trust signals travel differently

A brand identity that reads as premium in Dubai Marina doesn't automatically read as trustworthy in Accra or Kampala. Local payment badges, delivery transparency, and customer service that responds in-region matter more than polish. We've seen GCC brands port a beautiful, minimal Dubai-built site into a new African market and watch conversion collapse — not because the product was wrong, but because the site never answered the trust questions a first-time buyer in that market actually has.

Compare this to how the same brands treat India

It's worth noticing the double standard. Most Dubai brands we talk to already treat India as a serious market: they'll commission Hindi or regional-language creative, integrate UPI, and hire a local team before they launch. Nobody questions that investment anymore — it's assumed. Africa gets none of that same default respect, despite a trade relationship that's arguably deeper and a digital economy compounding just as fast. The instinct isn't wrong when applied to India. It's just selectively applied.

That gap is exactly why the opportunity is still open. The agencies and brands willing to extend the same localisation discipline they already use for India — market-specific payments, market-specific creative, a team that understands the buyer — to Kenya, Nigeria, Egypt or South Africa are competing in a market with a fraction of the noise.

What this looks like in practice

A Dubai-based retail or D2C brand eyeing Nairobi or Lagos doesn't need a from-scratch operation. It needs three things done properly: a payments layer that leads with mobile money and local gateways, not cards; a website and app built fast and lean enough to perform on the mid-range Android devices most of the market actually uses, not the flagship iPhones GCC teams design for by default; and a brand voice localised by market, not machine-translated from the Arabic or English original.

Take a hypothetical that mirrors conversations we're already having: a Dubai beauty or wellness brand with a strong regional following wants to test Kenya before committing further. The wrong move is shipping the existing Shopify store as-is and running the same Meta ad creative with the currency swapped. The right move is a lean, market-specific landing experience with M-Pesa integrated at checkout, delivery partners already active in Nairobi, and creative shot or adapted for a Kenyan audience — tested with a few thousand dollars of spend before any inventory or fulfilment commitment is made. That's a two-to-four week build, not a six-month market entry programme, and it tells you honestly whether the demand is there before you scale into it.

For service and B2B businesses already moving product or capital through the UAE-Africa corridor, the opportunity is simpler still: most haven't built any digital presence for the African side of their business at all. A logistics or trading company doing real volume into Kenya or Nigeria, running on WhatsApp and word of mouth, is leaving structural growth on the table — a proper website, CRM and lead system built for that market compounds the trade relationship that's already there instead of just servicing it.

The window won't stay open by accident

Every year the UAE signs another CEPA, every port DP World brings online, every African company that sets up in Dubai — the corridor gets easier to use and more competitive to enter. The businesses moving now aren't reacting to a trend; they're building on infrastructure that governments and logistics companies have already spent a decade putting in place. The ones who wait for Africa to show up in a "top markets to expand to" listicle will be building brand trust from zero in a market their competitors have already spent two years learning.

None of this requires abandoning the Saudi or Qatar roadmap — Africa doesn't have to compete with the rest of the expansion plan, it just deserves a place on it. The businesses that will look smart in three years aren't the ones betting the company on a single new market. They're the ones running a handful of small, disciplined tests across Kenya, Nigeria or Egypt in parallel with everything else, learning which one actually converts before a competitor gets there first.

Africa isn't the market after Saudi. For a Dubai-based business, it may already be the market next door.
